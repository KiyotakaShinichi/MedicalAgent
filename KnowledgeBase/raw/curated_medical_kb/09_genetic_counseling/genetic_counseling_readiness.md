# Genetic Counseling Readiness

## Purpose

This document supports OncoTrack's Genetic Counseling Readiness module. It is educational and organizational only. It must not diagnose inherited cancer risk, interpret a genetic variant as medical advice, predict whether a person or relative will get cancer, or replace a genetic counselor.

## What genetic counseling is

Genetic counseling helps a patient and family understand whether genetic testing may be useful, what a test can and cannot answer, and what questions should be reviewed with a genetics-trained clinician. It commonly uses personal cancer history, family cancer history, age at diagnosis, cancer types across relatives, ancestry when relevant, and prior known familial variants.

OncoTrack may organize this information into a review-ready summary. OncoTrack must not decide that a patient "has BRCA," "will get cancer," or does not need counseling.

## What information is useful to collect

Useful family-history details include relationship to the patient, maternal or paternal side, cancer type, age at diagnosis, whether more than one relative was affected, male breast cancer history, ovarian or pancreatic cancer history, prostate cancer history, and whether a familial pathogenic variant has already been documented.

Patients should avoid uploading another person's identifiable medical records unless they have permission. The safer workflow is to record non-identifying family-history facts and ask the care team what documentation is appropriate.

## When to route for clinician or genetic counselor review

Route for review when a patient reports a known familial pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant, multiple relatives with related cancers, ovarian cancer in the family, male breast cancer, early-onset breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, metastatic or high-risk prostate cancer in close relatives, or uncertainty about whether a genetic result is germline or tumor-only.

## Safe assistant wording

The assistant may say: "This family history may be worth discussing with your oncology team or a genetic counselor." It may also ask clarifying questions to organize the record, such as which side of the family the relative is on, the cancer type, and the age at diagnosis if known.

The assistant must not say: "You have inherited cancer risk," "Your relatives will get cancer," "You do not need a genetic counselor," or "This result decides your treatment."

## Questions a patient can ask a genetic counselor

- Based on my personal and family history, is genetic testing appropriate?
- If testing is recommended, should it be germline testing, tumor testing, or both?
- Would a multigene panel be useful?
- What would a positive, negative, or VUS result mean for me and my relatives?
- Who in my family, if anyone, should consider testing first?
- How should my genetic information be shared while protecting privacy?
